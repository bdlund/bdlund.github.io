AI literacy and explainability

My work on AI literacy asks what people need to know, and be able to do, to engage critically with AI systems. It treats AI literacy as a question of knowledge and power as well as skill: who gets to understand AI systems, question them, and shape how they are used.

The papers here approach the question from three directions. One reframes AI literacy through Foucault's power-knowledge and Freire's critical consciousness. One treats explainable AI as a scaffold that builds literacy while people use AI tools. One proposes how to teach explainability and social accountability to future AI developers in data science, computer science, and information science programs.

Papers and resources

AI Literacy: An Exercise in Power-Knowledge

Brady D. Lund, Zoë Abbie Teel · 2026 · Proceedings of the Ninth AAAI/ACM Conference on AI, Ethics, and Society (AIES 2026) · Preprint (author manuscript)

Most AI literacy frameworks teach technical skill and responsible use, which trains people to be consumers of AI output. This paper draws on Foucault's idea of power-knowledge and Freire's critical consciousness to describe AI literacy as a critical practice: evaluating AI systems, questioning their built-in assumptions, and taking part in how they are governed. It proposes a three-part framework of contextual use, critical interrogation, and participatory governance, and argues that unequal access to AI tools repeats older epistemic injustices that any literacy framework has to address.

Explainable artificial intelligence as AI literacy scaffolding

Brady D. Lund, Zoë Abbie Teel, Brett Porter, Anuradha Chandrasekaran, Sanket Ramchandra Patole · 2026 · Journal of Documentation · Accepted manuscript

Explainable AI is usually discussed as a way to deliver transparency and accountability. This paper treats explanations as learning support: a scaffold that helps users build AI literacy gradually while they work with a system. The framework rests on three principles: support matched to the learner's zone of proximal development, explanatory support that fades as competence grows, and explanations that serve as starting points for inquiry. The goal is calibrated trust, which avoids both uncritical acceptance and unwarranted skepticism. The paper also outlines methods for measuring scaffolding in user interactions with explainable AI.

Toward socially accountable data science education: Proposing a conceptual framework for integrating explainable AI (XAI) and accountability principles

Brady D. Lund, Kinza Alizai, Eunice Amoje, Anuradha Chandrasekaran, Stefan Darvischi, Jeanne Denmark, Nishanth Paulraj, Lalitha Nallamothula, Antonio Paes, Bavya Sri Vemulapalli · 2026 · Preprints.org · Preprint

This paper proposes a framework for teaching explainable AI and social accountability in data science, computer science, and information science programs. The framework has four pillars (answerability, responsibility, enforcement, and reflexivity) and shows how techniques such as LIME, SHAP, model cards, and counterfactual explanations can work as accountability tools. A multi-level governance framework links these methods to institutional oversight, regulatory literacy, and participatory design, illustrated with a scenario from graduate data science education.
