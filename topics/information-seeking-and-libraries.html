Information seeking and libraries

These studies look at how people find and use information now that generative AI sits alongside search engines and library resources, and at what library services should do about it.

A review of AI and health information seeking traces how AI is changing information-seeking theory and practice. Two surveys of U.S. university students compare AI chatbots, search engines, and library databases on use, preference, and satisfaction. A thematic analysis of BookTok comments uses the Berrypicking Model to show what public libraries can learn from online reading communities. A validated survey of academic librarians measures awareness of green library practices.

Artificial intelligence (AI) and information seeking: A comparative exploration of AI chatbots, search engines, and library resources as information sources among university students

Brady D. Lund, Zoë Abbie Teel, Yara Mohammed, Abhignya Jagathpally, Ting Wang · 2026 · Journal of Librarianship and Information Science · Accepted manuscript

A survey of 236 U.S. university students measured use, preference, perceived relevance, and satisfaction across AI chatbots, search engines, and library databases. Search engines such as Google remain dominant for academic information retrieval, and generative AI is an increasingly important part of students' information worlds. Younger and international students are especially likely to use AI for academic tasks, and frequent AI users report higher satisfaction with what AI provides. AI appears to act as both a complement and a competitor to search engines and library resources, which matters for information literacy instruction and academic library services.

Bringing Artificial Intelligence (AI) into Health Information Seeking Behavior: A Study of AI and Information Seeking Research

Brady D. Lund, Nishith Reddy Mannuru, Malavika Katta, Sesha Sai Leela Madhuri Hota, Akshaya Pamukuntla, Sravya Uppala, Sai Madhav Kola, Aashrith Mannuru · 2025 · Journal of Health Communication · Original manuscript

This paper examines how AI changes research and practice on information seeking, with a focus on health information. It traces the history of AI in information seeking from the 1950s to the present and argues that when a chatbot evaluates sources and returns a single answer, the person's own evaluation steps are replaced by the model's judgment. Existing theories, such as Bates' Berrypicking model, need rethinking because AI-mediated search is more linear, and AI literacy becomes at least as important as traditional information literacy. It draws out implications for health settings, including symptom checkers and chatbots, and for information professionals who will be asked to teach these skills.

Measuring university students’ satisfaction with traditional search engines and generative AI tools as information sources

Brady D. Lund, Scott J. Warren, Zoë Abbie Teel · 2026 · Reference Services Review · Preprint (submitted version)

A survey of U.S. university students in late fall 2025 (236 valid responses) measured how often they use, and how satisfied they are with, generative AI tools and traditional search engines. Cluster analysis found two groups: students highly satisfied with search engines but dissatisfied with AI, and students moderately to highly satisfied with both. Frequency of use strongly predicts satisfaction, and international and undergraduate students are more satisfied with AI than domestic and graduate students. Overall, students were more satisfied with search engines, and those who preferred AI tended to see it as a complement to other sources.

BookTok Boom and Public Libraries: A Thematic Analysis of ‘Fangirl Comments on TikTok, Berrypicking Behavior, and Implications for Public Library Services

Zoë Abbie Teel, Brady D. Lund, Ting Wang · 2026 · Public Library Quarterly · Original manuscript

The study applied thematic analysis, with Bates' Berrypicking Model as the framework, to the 50 most-engaged comments on each of five #BookTok videos. The most common comments were general information posts, expressions of emotional attachment to books, and comments that encourage interaction with other fans. The authors argue that public librarians can read these patterns as signs of reader motivation, emotional connection, and community, and use them in collection development, reader's advisory, and programming.

Green practices in academic libraries: validating a survey to measure sustainability awareness and perceptions

Zoë Abbie Teel, Brady D. Lund, Ting Wang · 2026 · Library Management · Preprint (submitted manuscript)

The study developed and validated a short survey of sustainability awareness and perceptions, using responses from 108 academic librarians in the United States. Factor analysis found two factors, awareness and perceptions, which together account for 63% of the variance, and both showed acceptable or better reliability. Librarians strongly supported sustainability values, such as libraries serving as environmental role models, but were less familiar with green ICT, sustainability theories, and technical systems. No demographic differences were found. The sample was self-selected, so it may overrepresent librarians who already care about sustainability.
