AI ethics, policy, and authorship

This research examines how institutions and people respond to generative AI: what universities write into their policies, what the public and information professionals think and fear, and how authorship should work when AI helps write.

A content analysis of 197 university policies shows which ethical themes dominate and which are missing. Interviews and a survey capture public perceptions of AI and anxiety about it among information science students and professionals. An essay on AI authorship proposes a model of responsible collaboration that keeps human intellectual ownership.

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) policy in higher education: A content analysis of ethical themes in the QS Top 200 university policies (2026)

Brady D. Lund, Bavya Sri Vemulapalli, Ting Wang · 2026 · Educational Management and Policy · Version of record (CC BY 4.0)

The study coded 197 publicly available generative AI policies from the 2025 QS Top 200 universities against a 16-theme AI ethics framework, and compared results by region, size, public or private status, and ranking quartile. Data bias, privacy, and security appear most often. Explainability, misinformation, and deepfakes are underrepresented. Policy is spreading quickly but remains fragmented, with inconsistent ethical coverage, uneven documentation expectations, and uneven support for instructors and students. To the authors' knowledge, no earlier study had analyzed policies across the top 200.

AI authorship and the role of the librarian

Brady D. Lund · 2026 · Theological Librarianship · Version of record (CC BY-NC 4.0)

This essay argues against treating AI-assisted writing as a choice between acceptance and prohibition. Building on the concept of bothorship, it proposes a model of responsible AI authorship that keeps intellectual ownership with the human author while recognizing the practical and equity benefits of AI-supported writing. It describes the role of libraries and librarians, including theological libraries, in teaching AI literacy, supporting ethical writing practices, and reducing inequities in scholarly communication.

Perceptions of the Fourth Industrial Revolution and AI’s Impact on Society

Brady D. Lund, Daniel Agbaji, Nishith Reddy Mannuru · 2024 · Perspectives on Global Development and Technology · Author manuscript

Semi-structured interviews with participants grouped by their exposure to information flows, analyzed thematically, show how people understand AI. Participants defined it with terms such as machine learning, automation, and replication of human intelligence. They saw benefits in problem-solving and efficiency, and raised concerns about job displacement, misinformation, data privacy, and copyright. Their views on government ranged from regulatory oversight to support for development. The authors conclude that AI literacy initiatives should be tailored to different groups and that policy should balance innovation with social accountability.

AI anxiety and fear: A look at perspectives of information science students and professionals towards artificial intelligence

Brady D. Lund, Nishith Reddy Mannuru, Daniel Agbaji · 2024 · Journal of Information Science · Accepted manuscript

A survey of more than 200 information science students and professionals examined how age, gender identity, ethnicity, geographic location, educational attainment, and residence relate to anxiety and fear about AI and the Fourth Industrial Revolution. Age, ethnicity, academic achievement, and regional context were the main differentiators. The authors suggest that students and professionals could benefit from further education about the technology.
